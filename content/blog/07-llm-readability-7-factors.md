# LLM Readability: The 7 Things I Now Check Before Publishing Anything

Here's a finding that genuinely stopped me in my tracks the first time I read it: a page that's objectively less authoritative or comprehensive than a competitor's can still win the citation in AI search, if the specific chunk of text a system pulls from it is better structured. Less relevant overall, more citable in the moment that actually counts.

That single idea reshaped how I think about "AI visibility" as a whole. It's not just classic ranking wearing a new outfit. A smaller or newer site really can out-cite a much bigger one, and I've seen it happen, which is exactly why I now check every piece of content against what's called LLM Readability before it goes live.

## Two Terms Worth Actually Knowing

LLM Readability is how easily a language model can process and pull meaning out of your content. Chunk relevance is a bit narrower, it's about how relevant one specific passage is to one specific part of a question, not the page as a whole. Put together: a page can be broadly excellent and still lose a citation if none of its individual chunks are clean enough to extract on their own. And a page that's only okay overall can still win if one of its passages nails a specific sub-question with real clarity.

## The 7 Things I Actually Check

**Natural language quality.** Basic clarity and grammar. If it's hard for a person to follow, it's usually hard for a machine too, for related reasons. This is the baseline, not the differentiator, but it's worth confirming before checking anything else.

**Structure.** Lists, tables, clear subheadings, a logical flow. This gives a system obvious boundaries for where one idea ends and the next begins. I've come to think of structure as doing extraction work in advance, rather than as a formatting nicety.

**Chunk relevance.** Each paragraph or section should have one clear job. I've caught myself cramming three sub-topics into one section more times than I'd like to admit, and it weakens all three.

**Matching actual intent.** Does the content directly answer the real question, or does it circle around the topic without landing anywhere? This one is deceptively hard to self-assess, since writers tend to assume their own intent is obvious from context that a fresh reader, or a machine, doesn't actually have.

**Leading with the answer.** State the conclusion first, then back it up. This flips a lot of people's natural instinct to build up to a point gradually, but it's how these systems actually weight the opening of a passage.

**Not too much throat-clearing.** Enough context that a passage makes sense pulled out on its own, without so much preamble that the actual useful bit gets buried. This is a genuine balancing act, and I check it specifically by reading only the first sentence or two of a section and asking whether the point has already landed.

**Staying consistent and specific.** Same name for the same entity, every time, plus concrete claims instead of vague ones.

## What This Looks Like Rewritten, For Real

Here's a passage I'd genuinely flag in a review: "There's a lot to consider when thinking about visiting Kyoto, and it really depends on what you're into, but generally speaking, many people find that certain times of the year work better than others for various reasons related to weather, crowds, and what's happening around the city at that time."

That fails basically every single factor at once. No structure, no direct answer, the actual recommendation never shows up, heavy pronoun use, zero specifics.

Here's how I'd rewrite it: "The best time to visit Kyoto is late March to early April, for cherry blossom season, or mid-November, for autumn foliage. Both windows draw the biggest crowds of the year. If you'd rather skip the crowds than chase peak scenery, June or September tend to work better, with mild weather and far fewer people at the major sites."

Leads with the answer, gives real date ranges, states an actual trade-off, and would hold up perfectly if a system pulled it out with zero surrounding context.

## Running the Same Passage Through All 7 Factors

It's worth actually walking through why the rewrite passes, factor by factor, since seeing the checklist applied makes it much easier to apply on your own content afterward.

1. **Natural language quality:** clean, grammatical, no awkward phrasing.
2. **Structure:** implicitly organized, the date ranges function almost like a short list even in prose form.
3. **Chunk relevance:** the whole passage has one job, answering "when should I visit," and does nothing else.
4. **Matching intent:** it directly answers the timing question rather than circling it.
5. **Leading with the answer:** the actual recommendation is the very first sentence.
6. **Minimal throat-clearing:** zero warm-up, it states the answer immediately.
7. **Consistency and specificity:** real dates, a real trade-off, no vague hedging language anywhere.

That's the difference between a passage that reads fine and a passage that's actually built to win a citation.

## Why This Genuinely Changed How I Pitch Smaller Businesses

I used to feel a bit stuck telling smaller businesses "authority takes years to build, be patient." That's still true for classic rankings. But this finding gave me something more honest to say: you're not only competing on how big or established your site is. You're competing, passage by passage, on whether your specific answer to a specific question is written more clearly than the equivalent passage on a bigger competitor's page. That's a genuinely fair fight, in a way domain authority almost never is, and I've watched smaller sites win it.

## A Quick Audit You Can Run on Your Own Content

Pick any page you consider strong. Read it section by section, and for each one, ask honestly: if this exact chunk were pulled out with zero surrounding context, would it still make complete sense and directly answer something specific? Anywhere the answer is no, that section is the highest-leverage place to start editing, well before touching anything else on the page.

## A Second Example, Because One Isn't Enough to Internalize This

Let me run through one more before-and-after, since I think this framework only really sticks once you've seen it applied a couple of times.

Weaker: "Pricing for our private tours can vary depending on a number of factors, including group size, duration, and the specific itinerary you choose, so it's a good idea to reach out for a personalized quote to get the most accurate picture."

Better: "Private tour pricing typically ranges from $150 to $400 per person, depending on group size and duration. Half-day tours (3-4 hours) start around $150 per person. Full-day tours (7-8 hours) start around $300 per person. Groups of six or more usually see a lower per-person rate."

The weaker version leads with hedging and defers the actual answer to a future conversation. The stronger version states real numbers immediately, breaks the variation into clear categories, and would make complete sense to a person, or a machine, encountering it with zero other context.

## Why I Now Treat This as a Pre-Publish Checklist, Not a One-Time Audit

I used to run the seven factors as an occasional deep audit on older content. These days I run through them, quickly, on every single piece before it goes live, the same way I'd run a spell-check. It takes a few extra minutes, and it consistently catches passages that read fine on a normal editorial pass but would fail to hold up as a standalone, citable chunk. Given how much of a site's actual AI visibility comes down to individual passages rather than whole-page impressions, that few minutes has become one of the better returns on time in my entire process.

## A Final Thought on Why Smaller Sites Should Actually Be Encouraged by This

I opened this post with a finding that genuinely surprised me, that a less authoritative page can out-cite a more authoritative one on the strength of better passage structure. I want to close on that same note, because I think it's the most useful takeaway in the whole framework for anyone working with a newer or smaller site. You don't need to win the authority game to start winning citations. You need to win the sentence-level game, on the specific passages that matter most, and that's a fight available to genuinely anyone willing to put in the editing work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is LLM Readability, in plain terms?**
It's how easily a language model can process, understand, and correctly represent your content, measured across seven things: clarity, structure, chunk relevance, matching intent, leading with the answer, not overloading with context, and staying consistent and specific.

**What's chunk relevance, specifically?**
It's how well one individual passage, not the whole page, answers a specific piece of a question. Because AI systems pull and cite passages rather than whole pages, a page can rank well overall and still lose citations if its individual sections aren't each self-contained.

**Can a smaller site really out-cite a bigger competitor in AI search?**
Yes, and I've seen it happen. Documents with lower overall relevance can still get cited if their specific chunks are more clearly structured or more directly relevant to the sub-question being asked.

**Is there a good target length for paragraphs?**
I aim for short, roughly under 400 characters for the passages I most want extracted, short enough to work as a clean, self-contained chunk on its own.

**Do all seven factors matter equally, or are some more important than others?**
In my experience, leading with the answer and chunk relevance tend to have the biggest impact relative to the effort involved. Natural language quality is more of a baseline requirement than a differentiator, since most published content already clears that bar.

**How is this different from writing a good meta description or summary?**
A meta description summarizes a whole page for a human deciding whether to click. LLM Readability is about individual passages inside the body content itself being independently extractable and citable, which is a different audience, a machine doing retrieval, and a different unit, the passage rather than the page.

**Should I apply all seven factors to every paragraph on a page?**
I focus the strictest version of this check on the passages most likely to answer a real, specific question, pricing, timing, key facts, comparisons. Purely transitional or introductory sentences don't need the same scrutiny, since they're not the passages a retrieval system is likely to pull anyway.
