# I Did Black Hat SEO When I Started. From Keyword Stuffing to Entity Clarity

I don't talk about this much, but I think it's more useful than pretending I arrived in this industry already doing things the right way. When I started in SEO, I did some genuinely black hat stuff. Keyword stuffing, mostly. Some hidden text. A footer crammed with every city name a business wanted to rank in, white text on a white background, technically invisible to a visitor and very visible to whatever crawler picked it up. I'm not proud of it, and I'm also not going to pretend it didn't happen, because I think the honest version of that story is more useful to someone starting out now than a version where I skip straight to "and then I always understood entities."

## What It Actually Looked Like Back Then

Keyword stuffing meant repeating your target phrase until a page barely read like language anymore. "Rome tour guide, best Rome tour guide, Rome tour guide prices, book a Rome tour guide today." Over and over, buried in a paragraph or, worse, in a block of text nobody was ever meant to actually read.

A few of the specific tactics I leaned on, since I think naming them plainly is more useful than being vague about it:

- **Keyword stuffing.** Repeating a target phrase far past what natural writing would ever produce, often mid-sentence, breaking grammar entirely.
- **Hidden text.** The same keyword-stuffed block, styled so a human visitor never saw it while a crawler theoretically still could, white text on white, font-size zero, text pushed off-screen.
- **Doorway pages.** Near-duplicate pages built for every city or keyword variation a business wanted, all funneling to the same actual offer underneath, existing purely to catch more search traffic through sheer volume of near-identical pages.
- **Exact-match domain stacking.** Registering several nearly identical domains stuffed with a target keyword, all pointing back to the same underlying business, purely to occupy more search real estate.

## Why It Worked, For A While

Here's the honestly uncomfortable part. It worked, for a while, because early search engines were mostly counting. How many times does this word appear. How many pages exist for this phrase. String matching, not meaning. A page that repeated "Rome tour guide" fifteen times had a genuine edge over one that said it twice, naturally, in actual sentences. I watched rankings move because of it, more than once, and at the time that felt like proof it was the right approach, not a warning sign.

## When It Stopped Working

It stopped being effective specifically because Google's own algorithm updates targeted exactly this.

**Panda, in 2011,** went after thin and low-quality content, pages that existed to catch search traffic rather than say anything useful. Doorway pages and content farms took the hardest hit.

**Penguin, in 2012,** went after manipulative link schemes and keyword stuffing specifically, the tactics I'd been leaning on directly.

I watched sites I'd worked on lose rankings within days of those updates rolling out, and it wasn't subtle. Whatever edge the stuffing had bought disappeared, and in a lot of cases the pages actually ranked worse than if they'd never been stuffed at all.

That was the moment it stopped being a strategy question and became a survival question. Keep doing what used to work and keep losing ground, or actually figure out what these updates were rewarding instead.

## The Shift That Actually Changed My Career

What Panda and Penguin were rewarding, it turned out, wasn't the absence of keywords. It was the presence of actual, specific, useful information, stated clearly, about a real, identifiable thing. That's a genuinely different skill than repeating a phrase, and it took me longer than I'd like to admit to unlearn the old instinct.

The real turning point for me was learning the Entity-Attribute-Value model, which I've written a full post about elsewhere on this blog. Instead of asking "how many times have I said the keyword," the question became "have I stated a specific, checkable fact about this specific entity."

### A Direct Before-and-After

**Before:** "Rome tour guide, best Rome tour guide, book today."

**After:** "Our Rome-based guides specialize in Ancient Roman history and hold official licenses issued by the Ministry of Culture."

Same underlying intent, a completely different piece of writing, and one that actually gives a reader, and now an AI system, something real to work with. That single reframe did more for my actual results than anything I'd learned in years of chasing keyword density.

## What This Taught Me About Shortcuts in General

Looking back, the actual lesson wasn't just "don't stuff keywords." It was broader than that. Any tactic built on gaming a proxy for quality, rather than actually producing quality, has a shelf life that's shorter than it looks while it's working. Keyword count was a proxy for topical relevance. Link volume, unqualified, was a proxy for authority. Both got gamed hard enough that the systems measuring them had to evolve past the proxy and toward something closer to the real thing. I think that pattern is still playing out today with newer tactics, and I try to keep it in mind whenever something feels like it's working suspiciously well for reasons that don't map to genuine usefulness.

## What I'd Tell Someone Starting Out Today

If you're new to this and you're tempted by anything that sounds like stuffing, cloaking, or doorway pages, even in some modernized version dressed up for AI search, I'd genuinely tell you to skip it. Not just because it's against the rules, though it is, but because I watched it stop working with my own eyes, in real time, on real accounts I was responsible for. The lesson from Panda and Penguin wasn't "don't get caught." It was "the whole approach was built on a shortcut that stopped existing."

What replaced it for me, and what I still lean on ten years later, is genuinely simple to state even if it's harder to execute well: say specific, true, checkable things about a specific, clearly identified entity. That's it. That's the whole shift, from stuffing a phrase to actually saying something.

## What I Do Differently Now When Reviewing Someone Else's Old Content

Because I lived through this myself, I try not to be harsh when I find old keyword-stuffed content on a site I'm newly auditing. It's rarely a sign of a bad marketer, it's usually a sign of someone doing exactly what worked at the time they learned the craft, the same thing I did. What I actually do is treat it as a straightforward, mechanical fix rather than a character judgment: identify the stuffed phrases, figure out what specific, checkable fact each one was actually trying to communicate, and rewrite toward that fact instead. The tone of that conversation matters more than people might expect, since a defensive business is a lot less receptive to fixing something than one that hears "this was normal for the time, here's what actually works now."

## The Part of This Story I Still Think About Most

What actually stays with me isn't the tactics themselves, keyword stuffing and hidden text are easy enough to describe and easy enough to stop doing once someone tells you to. What stays with me is how confident I was while doing it, because it was measurably working, in the moment, by every metric I had access to at the time. That's the part I try to pass on now: being right today doesn't mean you're right for the long run, and the discomfort of unlearning something that's currently working is a genuinely hard skill, harder than learning something new from scratch ever was for me.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the difference between keyword stuffing and normal keyword usage?**
Normal usage means your target phrase appears naturally where it belongs in a sentence that's actually saying something. Stuffing means repeating it well past what natural writing would ever produce, purely to catch a crawler's attention.

**Did keyword stuffing ever really work?**
Yes, genuinely, in the era before Panda and Penguin, when search engines were mostly counting term frequency rather than evaluating actual meaning and quality. It stopped working specifically because those updates targeted it directly.

**What replaced keyword density as the thing that actually matters?**
Stating specific, checkable facts about clearly identified entities, what I now think of through the Entity-Attribute-Value model. A page that says one true, specific thing beats a page that repeats a phrase fifteen times, by a wide margin now.

**Are there modern versions of these old black hat tactics I should watch out for?**
Yes, and honestly some newer ones dressed up for AI search, things like stuffing a page with synthetic questions purely to game synthetic query matching, or generating thin AI content at scale without any real information behind it. The underlying warning is the same one I learned the hard way: shortcuts that skip actually saying something real tend to stop working, eventually, and often faster than you'd expect.

**Did you ever get penalized directly, or did rankings just quietly drop?**
Both happened at different points, depending on the site and the update. Some pages just lost rankings gradually as the algorithm re-evaluated quality. Others took a much sharper, more obvious hit right when a specific update rolled out. Either way, the direction was the same, and it never reversed on its own.

**How long did it take you to fully unlearn the keyword-stuffing instinct?**
Longer than I'd like to admit, honestly, probably a couple of years of consciously retraining myself. The instinct to "just add the keyword again" doesn't disappear overnight. What helped most was having a concrete replacement framework, the Entity-Attribute-Value model, to reach for instead, rather than just being told to stop doing the old thing.

**Would you say Panda and Penguin were good things for the industry overall?**
In hindsight, yes, even though living through them at the time was rough. They forced a genuinely necessary shift toward content that actually serves readers, and I think the industry as a whole is healthier for it, even if a lot of us had to unlearn some uncomfortable habits to get there.

**Do you think there will be a future update that similarly reshapes how AI search evaluates content?**
I'd be surprised if there wasn't, honestly. Every major shift in how content gets evaluated has eventually been followed by another one, and AI search is still young enough that I expect real, structural changes ahead. I try to stay grounded in fundamentals, specific, accurate, well-structured content, precisely because those seem to survive each shift better than any specific tactic does.
