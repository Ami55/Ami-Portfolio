When a brand is described incorrectly in an AI answer, the immediate reaction is often, “The model hallucinated.” Sometimes it did. Sometimes the brand’s own website gave it several conflicting versions of the truth.

I have seen company names written differently across pages, outdated service descriptions, old leadership details, locations that appear and disappear, and About pages that do not match product pages.

## Retrieval needs a stable version of the brand

AI systems do not experience a website as a loyal customer does. They retrieve passages, compare sources, and try to connect names, attributes, and relationships.

If the same entity is described five ways, the system has more work to do. If external profiles repeat an old fact, confidence becomes even harder.

The fix is not to repeat the brand name unnaturally. It is to maintain a reliable source of truth.

## The audit starts with ordinary facts

I check the preferred name, short description, services, locations, leadership, founding information, contact details, and important relationships. Then I compare those facts across key pages, schema, social profiles, business listings, and other controlled sources.

This recurring work led to my [AI Hallucination Brand Fact Checker](https://seogirl-ai-hallucination-brand-fact-checker.vercel.app/). The tool helps expose contradictions; a person still needs to decide which statement is correct.

## What my five-brand study added

In [Who Are You, Really?](/research/brand-consistency), I checked four identity facts for Shopify, Hootsuite, Clio, Unbounce and Jane App across knowledge panels, ChatGPT, Google AI Mode and public web sources. The observations were collected on October 22, 2026.

Three of the five brands had conflicting founding years in the material reviewed. For two brands, a checked fact differed between the knowledge panel and AI Mode. These are dated observations, not claims about what those interfaces display today.

The important question was not which answer sounded most confident. It was whether the sources were describing the same fact.

A company can have an early project, an incorporation date and a public launch. Calling all three “founded” can create an apparent disagreement that a more precise history would resolve. In other cases, the statements really do conflict and need verification.

The study does not expose the internal source pipeline behind either Google feature. Different displayed facts do not by themselves prove why the discrepancy happened. They give me a mismatch to investigate.

## Define the attribute before choosing the value

For a fictional business, “founded in 2011,” “incorporated in 2012” and “publicly launched in 2014” could all be compatible. I would publish those distinctions only if records support them. This is an illustrative example, not a corrected history for one of the sampled brands.

Headquarters needs the same care. A registered address, an operating base, a regional office and a mailing address are not necessarily interchangeable. A global company can have several important locations without every location being its headquarters.

Leadership introduces time. A statement can be historically accurate and still be unsuitable for a question about the present. I would record the effective date and the date the evidence was checked, then verify a current claim before republishing it.

Those definitions should travel into structured data and external profiles where the fields actually support them. I would not force a nuanced history into an inaccurate single value merely to make all surfaces agree.

## Trace disagreement instead of voting on it

If four pages repeat one date and one primary record supplies another, I would not settle the issue by majority vote.

I would save the conflicting statements, identify their subjects and definitions, note their dates, and follow each claim to its evidence. Several pages may have copied the same biography. Agreement between them is then repeated publication, not independent corroboration.

Where the business controls the source, I would ask the appropriate owner to verify the claim. Where the source belongs to a third party, I would document the correction request rather than assume an edit is possible.

I would preserve unresolved cases. “Not verified” is more useful than a clean fact sheet containing a guess. A mismatch between a local-office listing and the company entity also deserves a different explanation from a wrong fact about the correct company.

## The identity record I would maintain

For each important claim, my record would include the entity's preferred name and identifier, the attribute's definition, the value, its scope, its evidence, the effective date, the last verification date and an owner. I would also list the pages and profiles using it.

That connects a plain-language identity block to a maintainable process. A paragraph is useful public copy; the supporting record explains why its claims should be trusted and how they will be updated.

The [Entity Clarity Workbook](/workbooks/entity-clarity) provides a practical starting point. My separate article on [brand-fact governance](/blog/brand-facts-source-of-truth) develops ownership, dates and correction workflows in more detail.

I would not promise that this work forces an assistant to adopt a preferred description. It gives the business a clearer, supported public account and makes later errors easier to diagnose. That is a concrete improvement even when an external answer remains outside our control.

## What I would check on Monday

- Write one approved brand fact sheet.
- Compare it with the About, contact, product, and location pages.
- Review Organization schema and controlled external profiles.
- Remove or explain conflicting legacy information.
- Assign ownership for keeping important facts current.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can inconsistent website facts cause incorrect AI answers?**
They can contribute to uncertainty. AI systems also use external sources, so consistency cannot guarantee a correct answer, but it gives the brand a clearer factual foundation.

**Is this only an AI search issue?**
No. Customers, journalists, partners, search engines, and support teams also depend on consistent brand information.

**Should every page repeat the same brand description?**
No. Context can change the wording, while core facts and entity relationships should remain consistent.
