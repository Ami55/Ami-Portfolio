# What Ten Years in SEO Changed My Mind About

I used to believe the best SEO specialist was the person with the fastest answer. Ten years later, I think the better specialist is often the person who asks one more question before recommending anything.

The work changed my mind about several things.

## More content is not automatically more opportunity

I have watched websites publish into weak architecture, unclear ownership, and overlapping intent. More pages made the problem larger. Sometimes the right recommendation is to consolidate, repair, or stop publishing long enough to understand what already exists.

## A technically correct audit can still be unusable

An audit is not finished when the crawler finishes. Someone needs to understand the pattern, business impact, owner, and reasonable next action. I now spend more time reducing recommendations than adding them.

## Rankings are evidence, not the whole outcome

Ranking matters, but so do discovery, indexation, useful clicks, conversion, entity understanding, and now retrieval and citations in AI answers. One number cannot describe the entire search system.

## Expertise sounds less certain than I expected

Experience has made me more comfortable saying, “This is what I observed,” “This is the most likely explanation,” and “Here is how I would test it.” That is not weakness. It is a more accurate description of the work.

## What has not changed

The useful questions remain surprisingly ordinary. What does the user need? What does the business provide? Can search systems understand the relationship? Can the team implement the recommendation? Did the change help?

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the biggest lesson from ten years in SEO?**
Context matters more than applying a standard checklist without understanding the site, team, market, and decision.

**Do SEO best practices still matter?**
Yes, as starting points. They still need prioritization and adaptation to the real system.

**Has AI changed the fundamentals of SEO?**
It has expanded how information is retrieved and presented, while clarity, evidence, accessibility, authority, and user usefulness remain important.
