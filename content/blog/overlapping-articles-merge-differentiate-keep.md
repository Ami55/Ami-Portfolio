Two articles appear for similar searches. The first recommendation is often to merge them.

I would pause before doing that.

Similar keywords can lead to different decisions. One reader wants to understand a problem. Another is choosing between solutions. Combining their pages might produce a longer article while making both jobs harder.

Before calling it cannibalization, I want to know what each page helps someone do.

## Start with the job, not the keyword

Imagine two fictional articles: “How to choose a school” and “Questions to ask during a school visit.” They share a subject and some vocabulary. But the first helps a parent establish criteria; the second helps them investigate a particular school.

I could make a case for keeping both, with a clear link between them. I would struggle to justify two general guides that repeat the same criteria in slightly different language.

For each URL, I would write one sentence: this page helps this reader make this decision. If the sentences are indistinguishable, I have a reason to investigate further.

## Overlap is a clue, not a diagnosis

I would look at query patterns, landing pages, internal links, and the actual content together. A shared query alone does not prove that one page is harming another.

Do the pages alternate for the same important searches? Does the less suitable page repeatedly attract visitors who need the other one? Are internal links using similar descriptions for two different destinations?

Those observations can help identify a problem. They still need context, including seasonality, changing intent, and how the pages have been edited.

This connects with [my article about changing search intent](https://www.seogirl.ca/blog/search-intent-does-not-stay-still). A sensible division of content two years ago may no longer reflect the decisions people are making today.

## Three decisions I would consider

{% table %}
- Decision
- When I would consider it
---
- Merge
- Both pages serve the same task, and their useful evidence belongs in one coherent answer.
---
- Differentiate
- Both have a reason to exist, but their titles, scope, or content fail to make that distinction clear.
---
- Keep both
- They already serve distinct audiences, decisions, locations, or stages with useful original material.
{% /table %}

I would not choose solely by word count. A short checklist can have a clearer purpose than a comprehensive guide, and merging it can remove the feature that made it useful.

## A merge should preserve the reason people used either page

Before merging, I would inventory the unique material: examples, research, explanations, downloads, and questions answered. Then I would decide what belongs in the surviving page and what deserves a separate resource.

The destination should make editorial sense. I would consider existing links, relevant traffic, conversions, and URL stability, rather than automatically keeping whichever article is newest.

If a URL is permanently replaced, an appropriate permanent redirect helps users reach the replacement and signals the preferred destination to Google. [Google’s redirect guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/301-redirects) explains the available methods.

I would update internal links and the sitemap, check the destination, and record the change date. Redirecting a useful article to an unrelated category page would not complete the editorial work.

## A canonical tag is not a substitute for deciding

Google describes canonicalization in the context of duplicate or very similar pages. It is a way to indicate a preferred representative URL, not a general instruction to combine the value of any two articles sharing a topic. [Google’s canonicalization documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls) is the starting point for that technical decision.

If both articles are distinct and useful, I would make their roles clearer. If one is being retired, I would plan the replacement. I would not add a canonical simply because an audit tool highlighted overlapping words.

## Check the combined outcome after the change

A surviving page gaining clicks does not automatically mean the merge succeeded. Some of those clicks may have moved from the retired page.

I would compare the combined before-and-after performance of the affected URLs, keeping comparable periods and query groups in mind. I would also check whether the user tasks we intended to preserve still have good answers.

For a differentiation project, I would look for clearer query-to-page alignment and useful onward navigation. For a merge, I would check redirects, indexing, and whether valuable sections or conversion paths were lost.

## What I would check on Monday

- Choose one pair of overlapping articles.
- Describe the reader and decision each serves.
- Identify unique evidence before recommending deletion.
- Choose merge, differentiate, or keep, with a written reason.
- Measure the affected pages together after making the change.

The goal is to make the website easier to use and maintain. Having fewer URLs is only helpful when the remaining content does the work better.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should articles targeting similar keywords always be merged?**

No. Similar wording can reflect different tasks or audiences. Compare the purpose and evidence before changing the structure.

**How would you differentiate two overlapping articles?**

Give each a specific reader task, adjust its title and scope, remove unnecessary repetition, and link between them where the next step is useful.

**Can a canonical tag fix content cannibalization?**

It is appropriate for duplicate or very similar content, not every case of topical overlap. Diagnose the editorial and technical situation first.

**What should I measure after merging articles?**

Review the combined performance of the affected URLs, technical implementation, and whether important user tasks and conversion paths remain supported.
