# Why I Actually Like SEO, After Ten Years of Doing It

People outside this industry tend to assume SEO is boring. Meta descriptions. Keyword research. Spreadsheets. I get why it looks that way from the outside. Ten years in, I can say pretty confidently that it's the opposite of boring, and I think the reason people assume otherwise is that they're picturing the paperwork instead of the actual job.

## It's Never The Same Job Twice

Here's the thing nobody tells you starting out: the rules genuinely change. Not occasionally, constantly. Google rolls out a core update and rankings shift under your feet, sometimes overnight. A new AI search feature launches and suddenly there's a whole new surface to think about that didn't exist the month before. I've been doing this for a decade and I still can't tell you with confidence what the landscape looks like in two years, and honestly, that's my favorite part of the job, not my least favorite.

Compare that to a lot of other disciplines where the core skill set is genuinely stable for years at a stretch. SEO doesn't give you that. You either keep learning or you fall behind, and there's no version of this job where you get to coast on what you learned five years ago.

### A Short List of What's Changed Just Since I Started

It's worth actually naming a few of these, because I think the pace is easy to underestimate until you list it out:

- Mobile-first indexing became the default, not an edge case.
- Core Web Vitals turned page experience into a measurable, ranking-relevant factor.
- BERT and MUM shifted ranking toward genuine language understanding, not just keyword matching.
- AI Overviews and chatbot search introduced an entirely new answer surface most content wasn't built for.
- Query fan-out and chunk-level retrieval changed what "being findable" even means at a technical level.

Every one of those was, at the time, a genuine "start learning something new" moment. I don't expect the list to stop growing.

## You Have To Actually Test Things

This is the part I think gets missed most. SEO isn't a set of rules you memorize and apply. It's closer to running a constant, low-stakes science experiment. You form a hypothesis, change something, and wait to see what actually happens, because Google doesn't publish a clean rulebook and even when it publishes guidance, the real-world effect on any specific site is never guaranteed to match what you'd expect on paper.

I've been wrong plenty of times. I've changed something confident it would help and watched it do nothing, or occasionally hurt. That used to bother me more than it does now. These days I treat a wrong guess as information, not failure, because the only way to actually learn how these systems behave is to test something real and watch what happens, not to theorize forever from the sidelines.

### How I Actually Run These Experiments

For anyone curious what this looks like in practice, it's usually some version of this:

1. Pick one specific, measurable change, not five changes bundled together where you can't tell which one mattered.
2. State the hypothesis plainly before making the change, so I can't quietly rewrite my own expectations after the fact.
3. Give it enough time to actually show an effect, resisting the urge to react to noise in the first few days.
4. Compare the result honestly against the hypothesis, including when it flatly disagrees with what I expected.
5. Write down what I learned, even when the answer is "inconclusive," because that's still useful information for the next test.

## The Puzzle Never Fully Solves

I like SEO for the same reason some people like puzzles that don't have one clean answer. You're working with a system you can't fully see inside, inferring how it behaves from the outputs it gives you, and adjusting your approach as those outputs shift. It's part detective work, part writing, part technical troubleshooting, and part genuinely understanding how another human is going to search for something and what they actually want when they do.

No two businesses present the same puzzle either. A tour operator's ranking problem looks nothing like a SaaS company's ranking problem, even when the underlying principles, entity clarity, structure, trust, are exactly the same underneath. Applying the same fundamentals to a completely different context, every single time, is what keeps it interesting a decade in.

## The Stakes Are Real, Which Makes It Matter

I don't think I'd have stayed this long if the outcomes were purely academic. When an SEO change works, actual revenue moves, actual bookings happen, an actual business does better because of something I recommended and tested. When a change doesn't work, that's real too, and I've had to sit with the people I work with and explain why something didn't move the needle the way I'd hoped. That weight is part of what keeps it from ever feeling routine. This isn't a hobby project with no consequences. Real businesses are depending on getting this right, and I take that seriously, ten years in, the same way I did on my very first real account.

## What Keeps Me In It

Honestly, it's the combination. The landscape keeps changing, which means I keep learning. The results are testable, which means I get to actually find out whether I was right instead of just believing I was. And the stakes are real, which means the work matters beyond my own curiosity about how search engines behave. I've met plenty of people who tried SEO for a year or two and moved on because they found it tedious. I think what they actually found tedious was memorizing rules that were about to change anyway. Once you stop trying to memorize a fixed rulebook and start treating this as an ongoing experiment, it stops being tedious almost immediately.

## What I'd Say To Someone Considering This Career

If someone asked me today whether SEO is a good field to get into, I'd tell them the truth: it rewards curiosity more than it rewards credentials, it punishes complacency faster than almost any other marketing discipline I know, and it's genuinely never finished. If that sounds exhausting, it's probably not the right fit. If it sounds like the most interesting part of the job, you'll probably like it here as much as I still do.

## The Moment I Realized I Wasn't Leaving This Field

A few years back, during a particularly disruptive update, I remember sitting with a genuinely frustrating stretch of unexplained ranking volatility, the kind where nothing in the public guidance matched what I was actually seeing across the sites I worked on. A colleague in a completely different field asked, not unkindly, why I'd want to keep doing something that unstable. I didn't have a clean answer in the moment, but sitting with the question afterward, I realized the instability was never actually the part I was tolerating. It was the part that had kept me interested for years already, and it's stayed that way since.

## Something I'd Say to My Younger Self

If I could go back and tell myself one thing at the start of this career, it'd be to stop looking for the moment things would finally settle down and become predictable, because that moment never comes, and expecting it just makes the constant change feel like a problem instead of the actual job. The people I've seen burn out fastest in this field are the ones still waiting for stability. The ones who stick around, myself included, are usually the ones who stopped waiting for it and started treating the instability itself as the interesting part.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Isn't SEO just following a checklist?**
There's a checklist layer, sure, technical basics, structure, clarity. But the actual skill is interpreting how a constantly shifting, mostly invisible system responds to changes you make, and that part is never just a checklist.

**How do you stay motivated when algorithm updates undo your work?**
I try to treat every update as new information about how the system actually behaves, not as a personal setback. Some of my most useful insights over the years have come directly from watching what an update changed and what it didn't.

**Is SEO still worth learning given how much AI search is changing things?**
In my experience, yes, because the underlying fundamentals, entity clarity, structure, trust, are proving remarkably durable even as the specific mechanics shift. The parts that made this job interesting ten years ago are largely the same parts making it interesting now.

**What's the most interesting part of SEO to you personally?**
Watching a genuine hypothesis play out. Deciding a specific change should help, making it, and then actually seeing whether reality agrees with me. That loop, more than any single tactic, is why I'm still doing this a decade later.

**What's the hardest part of SEO that people don't talk about enough?**
Sitting with genuine uncertainty. You rarely get a clean, confirmed answer for why something worked or didn't, and you have to make real recommendations anyway, based on the best evidence available rather than certainty. That's uncomfortable for a lot of people, and I think it's honestly the biggest reason some folks burn out on this field faster than others.

**Do you ever get tired of how much you have to keep relearning?**
Occasionally, sure, especially right after a big update forces a real re-think of something I thought was settled. But most of the time it's the opposite feeling, closer to relief that the job still has room to surprise me after ten years, instead of having flattened into something purely repetitive.

**What would make you actually leave SEO for something else?**
Honestly, the day it stopped changing. I don't see that happening anytime soon, given how much AI search is still evolving, but if this field ever settled into something static and fully solved, I'd probably lose interest fairly quickly, and I say that as someone who's genuinely surprised by how long it's kept my attention already.

**Do you think AI will eventually make SEO as a discipline obsolete?**
I don't think so, though I expect the specific tactics to keep shifting the way they always have. Someone still needs to make sure a business's content is discoverable, accurate, and trusted, whatever the retrieval mechanism looks like underneath. The name of the job might keep changing, as I've written about elsewhere on this blog, but the actual need behind it looks durable to me.
