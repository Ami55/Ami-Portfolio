# E-E-A-T and Topical Authority Are the Same Mechanism. It Took Me Years to See It

I wrote a whole post on this blog about the three levels of E-E-A-T, and a separate one about topical authority, and for a long time I genuinely treated them as two different subjects in two different parts of my brain. It wasn't until I sat with both frameworks side by side that I realized they're describing the exact same underlying thing from two different angles. That realization changed how I explain both of them to every business I work with now.

## The Signal That Connects Them

Buried in the domain-level part of E-E-A-T is a specific signal: topical focus and content originality across the whole site, judged as a pattern over time, not page by page. A domain that's consistently published original, accurate content in one coherent area builds a different kind of trust than one publishing broadly and thinly across unrelated subjects, even when the individual pages on that broader site are each well-written on their own.

That is the exact same thing topical authority calls cost of retrieval, just described from a different angle. A search engine looking at a topically coherent site already knows, from everything else it's seen there, what that site's expertise actually covers, and how much it can trust new claims within that area. A new page on a topic the site has never touched before starts from close to zero, no matter how many strong pages exist elsewhere on the same domain.

Same mechanism. One framework calls it a trust signal. The other calls it a retrieval efficiency concept. Once I saw that, a lot of strategy conversations got a lot simpler.

## Why This Isn't a Small, Linear Thing

If topical focus just added a little bit of trust with each new relevant page, in a straight line, this wouldn't be that big a deal strategically. But I don't think that's actually how it works. I think it compounds, more like a multiplier than a flat addition.

A tenth article on a subject you've already proven deep expertise in reinforces something already established, and needs very little independent verification. A first article on a subject entirely outside your usual focus starts from close to zero, regardless of how respected the rest of the site is. This is exactly why I've seen a narrow site with fifty genuinely coherent articles routinely beat a broader site with five hundred loosely related ones, on searches inside that narrow site's specific lane. Every new page on the narrow site is reinforcing an existing pattern. Every new page on the scattered site is closer to starting over.

## Where the Author Actually Fits

The source-entity level of E-E-A-T, individual author or organizational credibility, connects to this in its own way. Whether an author's actual background matches the specific subject of a piece, not just whether a byline exists at all, is a real, named signal in this framework.

Which means topical coherence has to hold at two levels at once for the full compounding effect to kick in. A site can be topically coherent overall while individual pieces are written by people whose backgrounds don't specifically match the sub-topic, and that weakens the author-level contribution even where the domain-level one is strong. On the flip side, one genuinely credentialed author writing consistently in their actual area of expertise, on an otherwise coherent site, reinforces both levels at once, and I've seen that combination outperform either factor alone, every time.

## Putting the Two Frameworks Side by Side

I find this easiest to explain with the two frameworks laid directly next to each other:

| E-E-A-T concept | Topical authority concept | What it actually means |
|---|---|---|
| Domain-level topical focus | Central Entity and cost of retrieval | A coherent site needs less verification work per new page |
| Source-entity credibility | Author matching Core/Outer content sections | Credentials only compound when they match the specific sub-topic |
| Trust as the overarching factor | Semantic Content Network cohesion | Both frameworks treat overall coherence as the thing that actually earns confidence |

## What I Actually Do With This Now

Before publishing outside an established core, I ask whether it's reinforcing an existing pattern or starting a brand new one from zero. Expanding into an adjacent topic isn't wrong, it's often necessary, but I plan for it costing more trust-building effort than a same-topic piece would, because that's genuinely how these signals seem to accumulate, not how people usually assume they do.

I also try to match authors to their real, demonstrated area of expertise consistently, instead of treating bylines as interchangeable across a site's full range of subjects. And I've started treating a genuinely new topical direction as needing its own runway, deliberately, rather than expecting a first piece on something unfamiliar to perform like the fiftieth piece on something established. That's not a flaw to work around. It's the mechanism doing exactly what it's supposed to do, and planning around it honestly has saved a lot of frustrated conversations with businesses expecting instant results from a brand new content direction.

## A Practical Test I Now Run Before Any New Content Direction

Before greenlighting a genuinely new topical area, I ask three questions in sequence: does this connect back to the site's Central Entity in a way I could explain in one sentence, is there a real, credentialed author whose actual background matches this specific new area, and am I planning enough runway, in both time and content volume, for this new direction to earn the same trust the existing core already has. If any of those three comes back weak, I either narrow the idea until it connects more clearly, or I set expectations accordingly before a single word gets written.

## A Concrete Illustration of the Compounding Effect

Picture two sites, both with fifty published articles. Site A has all fifty articles genuinely centered on one clear subject, written by two or three consistently credited authors whose backgrounds match that subject. Site B has fifty articles spread across a dozen loosely related subjects, with rotating, uncredited bylines throughout.

Even with identical article counts and comparable individual writing quality, I'd expect Site A to out-rank and out-cite Site B within its actual subject area by a wide margin, because every one of its fifty articles is reinforcing the same domain-level and source-entity signals, while Site B's fifty articles are each starting closer to zero, spread thin across a dozen different, barely-connected areas. This is the compounding effect in practice, not a theoretical curve on a graph, but a real, observable gap between two sites that technically look similar on a simple content-count basis.

## What This Means for How I Plan a Site's First Year

Given how much this compounds, I now advise new content operations to resist the temptation to cover a wide range of topics early, even when some of those topics look like easy traffic wins. Building deep, narrow coverage in one area first, with consistent, credentialed authorship, sets up the compounding effect to actually start working. Spreading thin across many areas early denies every one of those areas the runway it needs to build real trust, and in my experience, that mistake is much harder to unwind later than it would have been to avoid in the first place.

## Why I Think These Frameworks Get Taught Separately, Even Though They Shouldn't

My best guess for why E-E-A-T and topical authority usually get taught as separate subjects is that they came from different corners of the industry, one grew out of Google's own quality rater guidelines, the other out of independent patent research and practitioner frameworks. Different origins, different vocabularies, describing the same underlying mechanism from two angles. I don't think that's anyone's fault exactly, it's just how industry knowledge tends to develop in silos. But treating them as genuinely separate, rather than two views of one thing, is exactly what led me to spend years not seeing the connection that, once seen, seems almost obvious in hindsight.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does publishing on a new topic hurt my existing E-E-A-T?**
Not directly, but it doesn't inherit the trust you've already built in your established area either. It effectively starts its own accumulation process, which is why expanding into new topics usually takes longer to show results than publishing more in your established core.

**Is topical authority just another name for E-E-A-T?**
Not exactly, but they overlap heavily. E-E-A-T's domain-level topical focus signal and topical authority's cost of retrieval concept both explain why a search engine trusts content more within a site's already-demonstrated area than equally good content outside it.

**Does author credibility matter more than a site's overall topical focus, or the other way around?**
They work at different levels and reinforce each other rather than substituting for one another. A focused site with an author whose credentials genuinely match the subject is a stronger combination than either factor alone.

**Can a site that covers many different topics still build strong E-E-A-T?**
Yes, but in my experience it works best when each major topic area is treated as its own coherent sub-network, with consistent authors and real depth, rather than publishing broadly with no internal structure at all.

**How long does it typically take for a new topical direction to catch up to an established core?**
There's no fixed timeline I'd commit to, since it depends heavily on how aggressively the new area is built out and how credible the authors behind it are. What I can say is that it consistently takes longer than people expect, which is exactly why setting that expectation up front matters so much.

**Is it possible to have strong source-entity credibility without strong domain-level topical focus?**
Technically yes, but I've rarely seen it perform as well as the combination of both. A highly credentialed author writing on a site with no coherent focus still leaves the domain-level signal weak, which drags down the overall evaluation even with a strong individual byline.

**Where should a business start if both levels are currently weak?**
I'd start with the domain-level focus first, narrowing the site's actual content direction around a clear Central Entity, since that creates the structure author credibility can then reinforce. Adding a credentialed author to an otherwise scattered site helps less than doing both in the right order.
