# Brand Identity Blocks: How I Rewrite "About Us" Pages So AI Actually Understands the Brand

There are two different goals hiding inside "AI visibility," and I've watched plenty of smart people mix them up. One is getting a specific page cited as a source in an AI answer. The other is getting your brand mentioned or recommended when an AI system talks about your category at all, whether or not any single page of yours gets cited that particular time. A page can be highly citable without your brand ever getting named as an option. And your brand can become one AI systems reliably associate with a category without any specific page being the source. Different goals, different fixes.

The tool I now use constantly for the second one is something called a Brand Identity Block, and it's honestly one of the simplest, highest-leverage rewrites I do for the companies I work with.

## The Idea Underneath It

Your brand name needs to consistently show up alongside the right attributes, topics, and category words, across as many trustworthy sources as possible. That's it. That's co-occurrence, and it's the actual mechanism behind how a language model builds its sense of what your brand is.

A brand mentioned alongside "private tours," "local guide," and "personalized travel" consistently, across dozens of independent sources, builds a strong, stable association. A brand described inconsistently, sometimes "guided experiences," sometimes "tours," sometimes "activities," across different places, builds a blurrier one, even with a similar total amount of coverage.

## What a Brand Identity Block Actually Is

It's a specific way of writing a brand description, built to give clear context to language models and modern search engines, without disguising it as something technical or machine-only.

Here's the part that genuinely reframed how I write these: a language model doesn't need structured data or content dressed up to look machine-friendly. It needs clear, plain sentences, subject, then what the subject does, then what it relates to, with a descriptive detail added when it's useful. Normal grammar, used well, already produces exactly what a language model is looking to extract. The actual skill is in deciding which of those plain statements to make, deliberately and consistently, not in making the writing look more technical than it needs to be.

## The Two-Part Structure I Use

I split every Brand Identity Block into two sections. First, positioning: what category is this brand actually in, and what's the core offering. Second, attributes: what specific things differentiate how this brand operates within that category. That's the whole structure.

Within the attributes section specifically, I try to cover a consistent set of dimensions wherever they're genuinely true:

- **Geography or scope.** Where the business actually operates, specifically rather than vaguely.
- **Operating model.** How it actually works, employed staff versus independent contractors, direct sales versus a marketplace model, and so on.
- **Audience.** Who it's genuinely built for, not "everyone," which is functionally meaningless to a machine trying to extract a specific claim.
- **Differentiators.** What specifically sets the operating model apart from the obvious category default.

## A Real Before and After

Here's a version I'd genuinely flag from a draft: "We're passionate about helping our customers have amazing experiences. Our team works hard to deliver quality service you can trust."

That says almost nothing extractable. No category stated, nothing concrete, nothing a machine could confidently attach to the brand at all.

Here's what I'd rewrite it to: "[Brand] is a private tour platform connecting independent local guides with travelers who want personalized, small-group experiences. [Brand] operates in over 170 countries, with guides who are locally based and independently vetted rather than employed by the platform directly. [Brand] is known for private shore excursions, city tours, and day trips built for couples, families, and cruise passengers who'd rather skip the big group tours."

Every sentence there is a plain, factual, extractable claim, category, geography, how the operating model actually works, who it's for. Nothing inflated, nothing vague.

## Where This Actually Needs to Live

Writing this once on an About page and calling it done doesn't do much. Co-occurrence works across the whole information ecosystem a model draws from, not one page. I push every business I work with to get the same consistent language, not just the same facts, into their own site's relevant pages, third-party listings and directories, review platforms, press mentions, anywhere their brand gets described at all.

A few specific places worth checking deliberately, since they're easy to overlook:

- **Third-party directories and marketplaces.** Business listings often get filled in once, at signup, and never revisited even as the brand's actual positioning evolves.
- **Press and partner mentions.** Old bios and boilerplate descriptions used by press contacts or partners can drift out of sync with how the brand actually describes itself now.
- **Social profile bios.** Short, but still a co-occurrence signal, and often the most neglected of all the places a brand's identity gets stated.
- **Review platform business descriptions.** Frequently set once and forgotten, even on platforms with real traffic and real AI-crawling activity.

Lightweight schema, Organization, Article, WebPage types, can reinforce this too, and it's genuinely worth adding. But it's a supplement. The plain-language block is what an AI system actually reads and reasons over, and I've stopped treating schema as a substitute for getting the sentences themselves right.

## How I Actually Roll This Out

When I take this on for a business, the process usually looks like this: draft the two-part block first, positioning then attributes, and get it approved as the single source of truth for how the brand describes itself. Then audit every place the brand gets described, on-site and off, for consistency against that block. Then update what can be directly controlled, and flag what can't for outreach. And finally, revisit the whole thing periodically, since brands evolve, and a Brand Identity Block that was accurate two years ago can quietly drift out of sync with what the business actually does now.

## A Mistake I Made Early With This Approach

The first few times I built these, I made them too promotional, still leaning on words like "leading" or "premier" even while trying to write plainly. Those words don't carry any extractable information, a machine can't do anything useful with "leading," since it's not a checkable claim, and worse, it crowds out space that could hold an actual, specific fact. I've since made it a hard rule for myself: every sentence in a Brand Identity Block has to survive being read as a claim someone could actually verify or check. If it can't be checked, it gets cut, no matter how natural it feels to include.

## How This Interacts With Everything Else I've Written About

A Brand Identity Block isn't a standalone tactic, it's the plain-language foundation the rest of the framework builds on. The Entity-Attribute-Value structure I've written about elsewhere on this blog gives the block its actual content, category as type, geography and operating model as attributes. The consistency principle from named entity recognition is exactly why the same block, worded the same way, needs to show up everywhere the brand gets described, not just once on an About page. And the co-occurrence idea underneath all of it is really just Brand Identity Blocks applied at scale, across every place your brand's name and its real attributes appear together.

## The Long Game This Actually Is

I'll be honest that this isn't a fast tactic, and I don't pitch it as one. Co-occurrence builds up gradually, across many independent sources, over real time. A single Brand Identity Block published once doesn't move much on its own. What actually works is the same consistent, specific description showing up, patiently, across dozens of places over months, so that by the time a language model is drawing on a wide swath of the web to form its sense of a brand, that consistent signal has had enough surface area to actually register.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the actual difference between LLM Readability and Brand Context Optimization?**
LLM Readability is about getting a specific page cited as a source. Brand Context Optimization is about getting your brand consistently mentioned or recommended when AI discusses your category, whether or not any specific page gets cited. I treat them as two separate jobs.

**What is a Brand Identity Block, exactly?**
A structured brand description written in plain, clear sentences using simple subject-then-relationship-then-detail statements, meant to give language models unambiguous context about your category and attributes.

**Do I need schema markup for this to work?**
It helps, but it's not the core mechanism. The real driver is your brand name consistently appearing alongside the right attributes across many places. Schema reinforces that for search engines specifically, it doesn't replace the plain-language writing.

**How is this different from normal marketing copy?**
Most marketing copy leans on subjective praise, "amazing," "trusted," "world-class," that carries nothing a machine can actually use. A Brand Identity Block states specific, concrete claims instead: category, geography, operating model, audience, as plain facts a language model can reliably parse and repeat.

**How long should a Brand Identity Block actually be?**
Usually three to five sentences is enough to cover positioning and the key attributes without diluting the signal. Longer isn't better here, specific and consistent matters more than comprehensive.

**How often should this be updated?**
Whenever the actual business changes in a material way, new geography, a different operating model, a repositioned audience, and otherwise on a periodic review basis, since consistency across sources tends to degrade quietly over time if nobody's checking.

**Does this replace the need for a normal brand style guide?**
No, they serve different purposes. A style guide covers tone, visual identity, and voice. A Brand Identity Block is narrower and more literal, the specific, factual sentences describing what the brand is and does, written for how a machine actually extracts meaning rather than how a designer thinks about brand feel.

**Can a Brand Identity Block hurt a brand if it's too specific?**
I haven't seen that happen in practice, provided the specifics are accurate. What can hurt is overstating something, claiming a geography or capability the business doesn't actually have, since that creates exactly the kind of factual inconsistency that undermines trust once it's cross-checked against other sources.
