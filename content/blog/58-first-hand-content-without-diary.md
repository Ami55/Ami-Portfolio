# How to Use First-Hand Experience Without Turning the Article Into a Diary

Some writers avoid first-hand experience because they do not want the article to become self-focused. That concern is reasonable.

The answer is not to remove the experience. It is to give every personal detail a job.

## Begin with the reader’s decision

Before adding a story, I ask what the reader needs to understand. If the story explains a risk, tradeoff, process, exception, or realistic expectation, it probably belongs. If it only proves that the author was present, it may not.

A strong first-hand passage can be short:

“During a sitemap audit for a large travel marketplace, I found more than 40,000 canonical pages missing from the sitemap files. The XML was valid; the generation rules no longer represented the site.”

That experience establishes the situation and introduces a practical lesson. The rest of the article can focus on the reader.

## Use a simple structure

I often organize experience in five parts:

1. The situation and its relevant conditions.
2. What I expected before investigating.
3. What I observed or measured.
4. What changed in my understanding or process.
5. What the reader can check in their own context.

This keeps the experience connected to a useful outcome.

## Specificity matters more than drama

First-hand content does not need a dramatic success story. A small operational detail can be more valuable than a large claim. Readers often need to know which export was compared, what pattern revealed the problem, or why the first attempted fix failed.

## Protect people and confidential information

Experience should not expose a client, colleague, customer, or private business detail. Remove identifying information when it is not necessary. Obtain permission for quotes and case details. Keep the lesson while respecting the source.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much first-person writing is too much?**
It becomes too much when the author’s story repeatedly interrupts the reader’s question instead of helping answer it.

**Does every article need a personal story?**
No. Use first-hand material where actual experience improves accuracy, usefulness, trust, or decision support.

**Can a short observation count as first-hand experience?**
Yes. Its value comes from relevance and specificity, not length.

**Can company experience be anonymized?**
Yes, when identifying details are unnecessary. Keep enough context for the observation to remain meaningful and avoid implying unsupported generality.
