A comprehensive guide can answer twenty questions and still make one important answer difficult to find.

A short article can get straight to the point and leave out the condition that makes its advice accurate.

That is why I would be careful about turning “short content gets cited” into an editorial rule.

For a travel marketplace, the more useful question is: **what needs to stay together for the traveller to make a sound decision?**

That is where I would start before shortening a guide or dividing it into separate pages.

## What “lost in the middle” actually tells us

The Lost in the Middle study examined how language models used information placed at different positions within their input context. In its tested tasks, performance often declined when relevant information appeared in the middle of a long context rather than near the beginning or end. [Read the original study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172).

That is an important finding about the models and evaluation settings studied. It does not establish an ideal webpage length or prove that splitting an article increases citations.

A webpage’s layout and the context supplied to a model are also different things. A retrieval system may select an excerpt, combine material from several sources, or transform content before it reaches the model.

I would take the research as a reason to make important information easy to identify and interpret. I would not use it as a reason to cut every article below an arbitrary word count.

## Separate the page’s purpose from the passage’s purpose

Consider a hypothetical guide to planning a cruise stop.

The page might help a traveller coordinate arrival, transport, sightseeing, and return timing. That is a connected planning task.

Within it, a section about meeting a private guide might answer a narrower question.

Both can be useful. The page provides the wider decision context; the section provides a focused explanation.

Before creating another URL, I would check whether that section has:

- A heading that describes its actual subject.
- A direct answer near the beginning.
- The conditions needed to interpret the answer.
- Evidence or a route to verify changing details.
- A clear connection to the wider planning task.

A focused passage does not always need to become a separate article.

## When I would create a specialized page

Some questions deserve their own treatment.

Suppose a general destination guide briefly mentions wheelchair access. Answering that topic responsibly may require detailed information about surfaces, gradients, transport, facilities, and the limits of what has been verified.

That could justify a dedicated resource.

The reason would be the distinct information need and the depth required to satisfy it.

I would be more likely to split content when the new page has its own audience task, useful evidence, and enough substance to stand independently. I would also consider whether a different person needs to maintain it.

A destination overview and a frequently changing port-transfer guide may need different review processes. Separating them can make ownership clearer.

## When splitting would make the answer worse

Now consider a cancellation explanation.

One paragraph describes the deadline. Another explains the time zone. A third covers exceptions.

Turning those into separate pages could force readers to assemble the policy themselves. An extracted answer might retain the deadline while losing the exception.

The same risk appears with hotel offers. A statement about breakfast inclusion needs to remain connected to the relevant room or rate. A tour’s accessibility information needs to remain connected to the route and departure it describes.

I would keep details together when separating them makes a claim easier to misunderstand.

Shortness is not useful if it removes the qualification that makes the answer true.

## A specific title helps set expectations

A specialized page can have a precise title and introduction. That helps the reader understand what it answers.

But precision comes from scope.

A long page can stay focused on a difficult problem. A short page can drift between several unrelated subjects.

I would review the title against the actual content:

Does the page deliver the promised answer? Does the introduction establish the relevant audience and conditions? Does the rest of the page support that purpose?

Google’s guidance for its AI search features continues to emphasize established SEO practices and useful, accessible content. It does not prescribe a special short-page format for inclusion. [Google’s AI features guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features).

I would therefore improve relevance directly rather than treating reduced length as a substitute.

## More URLs do not necessarily mean more source diversity

Another argument for splitting content is that AI systems may seek a variety of sources.

I would not turn that into a universal rule that an LLM avoids citing the same document repeatedly. Citation selection depends on the system and its implementation.

There is also a distinction between additional pages and additional perspectives.

Five articles from the same publisher, repeating the same evidence, do not automatically provide five independent sources. Creating more URLs does not guarantee more citations.

If a separate page contributes original evidence or answers a genuinely different question, it has a reason to exist. If it merely relocates a few paragraphs to create another citation opportunity, I would question the value.

## The decision framework I would use

Before splitting an existing guide, I would assess the proposed page against these questions:

{% table %}
- Question
- What it helps determine
---
- Does it serve a distinct reader task?
- Whether the scope deserves a separate page
---
- Can it stand alone without losing essential context?
- Whether separation improves understanding
---
- Does it add enough evidence and explanation?
- Whether it is more than a fragment
---
- Does it need different maintenance or ownership?
- Whether separation helps keep it accurate
---
- Will it substantially repeat another page?
- Whether the new structure creates unnecessary overlap
{% /table %}

I would then choose among three actions: improve the existing section, create a dedicated resource, or remove repetition.

The answer may differ across the same website.

## How I would test the change

I would begin with a small number of pages and specific customer questions.

Before editing, I would record where the relevant information lives and whether a reviewer can find a complete, accurate answer.

After restructuring, I would repeat that exercise. Did the answer become easier to locate? Were important conditions preserved? Does the new page offer something useful independently?

AI citation checks could provide additional observations, but they would need consistent prompts and repeated checks. A new citation after a split would not, by itself, prove that the split caused it.

I would also watch whether the change created maintenance problems: duplicated facts, conflicting updates, or several pages competing to answer the same question.

## The content structure I would aim for

For a travel marketplace, I would usually want useful overview pages connected to specialized resources where the subject warrants them.

The overview helps someone understand the decision. The specialized resource provides the detail needed to resolve a particular part of it. Each section keeps its claims and qualifications close together.

That gives me a more practical editorial principle than “long” or “short”:

**Make each page complete for its purpose, and each important passage clear enough to be understood accurately.**
