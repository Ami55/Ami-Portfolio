Imagine removing the destination name from a travel article.

Would the advice still work almost anywhere?

“Explore the local culture.”\
“Wear comfortable shoes.”\
“Book early to avoid disappointment.”

Those sentences might be reasonable. They also leave most of the traveller’s real questions unanswered.

When I assess information gain in travel content, I would ask what uncertainty the page removes.

I am using “information gain” here as an editorial principle: the useful knowledge a reader gains beyond what they already had. I would not present it as a score we can calculate to predict Google rankings.

## Start with the unresolved decision

Consider a hypothetical cruise passenger choosing between two walking tours.

Both visit the same landmarks. Both have strong photographs. Both promise a knowledgeable guide.

The traveller’s concern is more specific: can they complete the tour comfortably and return to the port before their required boarding time?

Another paragraph about the city’s history may add interesting information. It does not necessarily help with that decision.

Useful details might include the walking distance, sections with stairs, the actual meeting location, transport arrangements, and what happens if the ship arrives late.

The strongest addition depends on the question.

## Useful detail needs context

A number can look precise while remaining difficult to use.

“The walk takes 20 minutes” raises several questions. From which exit? At what pace? Does the route include stairs? Was it checked recently?

I would prefer a bounded observation that explains its conditions.

For example, a hypothetical field note could record the route, the date checked, the approximate distance, and the presence of a stepped section. It should not promise that every traveller will complete the route in the same time.

That is where first-hand information becomes valuable: it helps a reader interpret the observation.

## Build a repeatable collection process

A marketplace does not need every writer to visit every property or experience. It does need a dependable way to collect useful knowledge from people who can verify it.

I would give suppliers a short set of questions tied to recurring customer uncertainty:

{% table %}
- Customer question
- Evidence to collect
---
- Can I bring a stroller?
- Route surfaces, steps, storage arrangements
---
- Is the room quiet?
- Room position and specific sources of noise
---
- Is pickup included?
- Included area, meeting instructions, exceptions
---
- What happens in bad weather?
- Operator’s current operating and cancellation policy
{% /table %}

Each answer needs a source and a review date. An editor should distinguish observation, policy, opinion, and marketing language.

That distinction matters particularly when a supplier’s claim sounds broader than the evidence supports.

## Add exceptions that change the choice

Sometimes the most useful information is a limitation.

A hotel can be convenient for the station but awkward for someone with heavy luggage. A tour can be excellent for adults but unsuitable for a child who cannot manage its walking requirements.

Explaining that limitation can help the right traveller select the experience and help another traveller avoid a poor fit.

Google’s guidance encourages original information, substantial value beyond source material, and content that helps readers achieve their goals. It does not prescribe a particular word count. [Google’s people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content).

## Measure whether the uncertainty decreased

I would test the revised page with the questions that prompted the work.

Can a reader now identify the correct option? Can they explain a relevant limitation? Do they still need to contact support for information the page should provide?

Support enquiries, booking misunderstandings, and usability sessions can help reveal whether the content is doing its job.

More words are easy to produce. A verified detail that changes a traveller’s decision takes more work—and gives the page a clearer reason to exist.
