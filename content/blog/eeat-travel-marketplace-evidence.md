A guide profile has a photograph, a biography, and ten years of experience.

The tour page has detailed copy, customer reviews, and a clear itinerary.

The marketplace has an About page and recognizable branding.

Those are useful starting points. But I would still want to ask a more specific question: **what evidence supports the claims a traveller is about to rely on?**

If the page says a tour is suitable for someone with limited mobility, who checked that? If it describes a guide as a specialist, what is the relevant experience? If the cancellation terms change between the listing and checkout, which version should the traveller believe?

This is where E-E-A-T becomes useful to me as an audit framework. It helps connect the quality of a page with the people and processes behind it.

## How I would read an E-E-A-T signals infographic

Olaf Kopp’s infographic groups possible quality signals into three levels: the document, the domain, and the source entity—the person or organisation behind the information.

That is a useful way to organize an investigation. It also needs careful interpretation.

The infographic brings together proposed signals informed by patents and other research. It should not be treated as a confirmed list of Google ranking factors. A patent can describe a possible technique without establishing whether, where, or how Google currently uses it.

Google’s own guidance says E-E-A-T is not a specific ranking factor. Its systems use a mix of factors that can identify qualities associated with it, and trust is the most important aspect. Search quality raters evaluate results but do not directly control rankings. [Google’s E-E-A-T guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content#eat).

For my work, the useful question is therefore: which parts of this framework help reveal a real weakness in the customer’s information?

## Document level: can the traveller verify the claim?

Imagine a hypothetical walking-tour listing that says:

> An easy route suitable for everyone.

The sentence is reassuring. It is also difficult to evaluate.

“Easy” could refer to distance, pace, elevation, or the guide’s willingness to stop. “Everyone” makes a much broader promise.

I would want the page to describe the conditions that matter: walking distance, surfaces, stairs, available breaks, and any limitations confirmed by the operator.

That would give the traveller a basis for making a decision.

The same approach applies to a hotel page. “Quiet rooms” becomes more useful when the information identifies which rooms, what potential noise sources exist, and the basis for the description. A guest’s observation from one stay should remain an observation, rather than becoming a permanent promise about every room.

This is also how I would approach information gain. The valuable addition is a verified detail that resolves uncertainty.

## Domain level: does the rest of the website support the answer?

A strong page can still sit inside an inconsistent system.

Suppose a tour listing promises free cancellation until a stated deadline. The help centre describes a different rule. The booking flow introduces another condition.

Adding an expert byline would leave the main problem unresolved.

I would trace a sample booking through the website and compare the statements a traveller encounters. Where a general policy has exceptions, those exceptions should be attached clearly to the relevant offer.

My audit would examine:

- Whether listings and checkout describe the same inclusions.
- Whether general policies distinguish supplier-specific conditions.
- Whether the business explains who operates the experience.
- Whether outdated pages continue to contradict current information.
- Whether a customer can find the correct support route.

I would use these checks to assess consistency and accountability. I would not label the result a Google “domain E-E-A-T score.”

## Source entity level: who knows this, and what did they contribute?

On a marketplace, the person writing the page may not be the person with the most relevant knowledge.

An editor may produce the description. A guide may know the route. An operator may control departure arrangements. A customer-service team may understand the recurring misunderstandings.

I would make those contributions explicit where they help the reader assess the information.

For example, a route description could identify that its practical details were reviewed by the operator, if that review actually happened. A destination article could explain the relevant experience of its contributor.

The scope matters.

A guide’s knowledge of one city does not automatically support every statement about national entry requirements. A supplier can confirm its pickup arrangements without being an independent judge of its own comparative quality.

The question is more precise than “Does this person look credible?” It is “Why is this source appropriate for this particular claim?”

## What I would do with the newly proposed signals

The infographic’s additions are interesting research prompts. I would translate them into editorial questions before turning them into recommendations.

- Total articles written by an author
- Does the publication history show relevant, substantive work?
---
- Proportion of evergreen articles
- Does the author maintain useful material as facts change?
---
- Average content length
- Does the content explain what the reader needs, without padding?
---
- Anchor-text n-grams
- How do linking pages describe this resource, and is that description accurate?
---
- Links arriving long after publication
- What keeps making the resource useful enough to reference?
---
- Similarity to authoritative seed sites
- Which observable editorial practices would improve our own work?
{% /table %}

These are my proposed audit questions, not interpretations of a verified Google scoring formula.

An n-gram is simply a sequence of adjacent words. Seeing that term in research would not persuade me to prescribe exact phrases for other websites to use when linking.

Likewise, the number of published articles would not justify increasing production without considering their value. A large archive can contain repeated generalities; a smaller one can contain carefully maintained expertise.

## Evergreen content still needs maintenance

A guide to choosing a private tour might remain relevant for years. Its examples, policies, and practical advice can still become outdated.

I would separate the durability of the topic from the freshness of the facts.

For a marketplace, that means identifying which statements need periodic review and which need an update when an operational change occurs. Meeting points, inclusions, accessibility information, and cancellation conditions deserve particular attention because they can affect the booking decision.

Changing the displayed date is not evidence that this work happened. Google also cautions against refreshing dates without substantial changes and says it has no preferred word count. [Google’s content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content#avoid).

A useful update should leave a clearer or more accurate page behind it.

## The audit output I would actually want

I would start with a small sample across important page types: a destination page, a tour listing, a supplier profile, an editorial article, and a policy page.

For each, I would record:

- The important claim.
- The evidence supporting it.
- The source qualified to confirm it.
- Where else the claim appears.
- Who owns corrections.

Then I would prioritize the gaps by their consequences for the traveller.

An unsupported accessibility claim deserves a different response from an incomplete author biography. A contradiction affecting the price or cancellation terms needs a clear operational owner.

This approach produces specific work: verify a statement, narrow a claim, correct a template, identify a contributor, or reconcile conflicting policies.

## What I want E-E-A-T work to achieve

I want a traveller to understand why the information is credible, where its limits are, and who is responsible for it.

That requires more than adding visible trust elements to a page. It requires evidence that can survive a closer look.

Kopp’s infographic is useful when it encourages that investigation. The practical value comes from identifying a gap and fixing it—not from treating every possible signal as another box to tick.

On a travel marketplace, the strongest question I can ask is straightforward:

**Would I be comfortable asking somebody to make a booking based on this claim and the evidence we provide?**
